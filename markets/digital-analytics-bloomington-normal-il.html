A monthly report goes out showing conversions up 40%, and the first reaction in the room isn't "great work" - it's "says who?" A spike like that six months earlier turned out to be the same event firing twice on every page load. Once that happens once, every good number after it gets read with one eyebrow raised, no matter how accurate it actually is.

It's rarely one dramatic failure. A tag gets installed during a launch, a handful of goals get configured, and the site keeps changing around it from there. A redesign swaps out a button, a form moves to a different platform, a new script gets added for something unrelated - and nothing checks whether tracking still lines up with what changed. None of it breaks all at once. It erodes, one small mismatch at a time, until the numbers and reality quietly stop agreeing.

Properly built tracking starts from what a business actually needs to know, not from whatever a default install happened to capture. Events get mapped to specific actions - a form submitted, a call started, a purchase completed - and reporting gets narrowed around those numbers specifically. The goal isn't a longer dashboard. It's a smaller set of numbers that hold up when someone asks where they came from.

That starts with a look at what's tracked today: what's firing correctly, what's counting the same action twice, and what quietly stopped working weeks ago without anyone noticing. From there, tracking gets organized around the handful of actions that actually matter to the business, rather than capturing everything and sorting it out later. A service business is usually watching qualified leads and phone calls. A retailer is watching completed orders and where carts get abandoned. The setup reflects that difference instead of treating every site the same.

Regular checks keep it that way. Browser changes, privacy settings, and new scripts added to a site can all interfere with tracking quietly, without throwing an obvious error. Catching a broken event in the first week it goes quiet, instead of at the end of a quarter, is the difference between a small fix and a month of reporting nobody can trust.

Reporting itself is built around what a business actually checks, on a schedule that matches how decisions get made - weekly, monthly, or tied to a specific push. Rather than a wall of charts nobody opens twice, the focus stays on the small set of numbers that explain what's working, what isn't, and what's worth a closer look.

For businesses in Bloomington-Normal and across McLean County, this kind of work is available locally, with the option to meet in person and walk through current tracking to see what it's actually capturing. Most engagements start by reviewing what's already in place, since a setup that's mostly working usually needs targeted fixes rather than a full rebuild.
