Cart Abandonment Is a Measurement Gap, Not a UX Problem

By Scott Taylor

Back to Insights

Knowing where a shopper left the checkout isn't the same as knowing why. Most setups only ever answer the first question.

Where the Analysis Usually Stops

A typical cart abandonment report shows a funnel: this many people added something to the cart, this many reached checkout, this many completed a purchase. It's genuinely useful for spotting which step loses the most people. It's also, on its own, a description of what happened without any explanation of why.

Step drop-off tells you where someone left. It doesn't tell you whether they left because of a shipping cost that surprised them, a form field that failed silently, an account requirement they didn't want, or simply a change of mind unrelated to the site at all. Those are completely different problems with completely different fixes, and a funnel report treats them all identically.

Why "Where" Isn't Enough on Its Own

Redesigning a checkout step based only on where people drop off is a guess dressed up as data. It might land on the actual cause. It might just as easily "fix" a step that was never the real problem, while the actual friction - say, a shipping estimate that only appears after an email address is entered - stays exactly where it was.

The step with the highest drop-off is real information. It's just half of it.

What the Other Half Actually Requires

Getting to "why" usually means tracking more specific signals than a standard funnel captures by default - whether a shipping or tax estimate loaded before someone left, whether a specific form field triggered repeated correction attempts, whether an account-creation prompt appeared right before the drop. None of this is exotic to set up. It's just rarely set up by default, which means most sites are missing it entirely rather than choosing not to have it.

Session recordings and heatmaps can supplement this, but they don't replace it - watching a handful of sessions shows what happened in those specific cases. Structured event tracking shows the pattern across everyone.

A Different Question to Start With

Instead of "where do people abandon," a more useful starting question is "what's the last thing most abandoning sessions have in common right before they leave." That reframes the analysis from a single funnel step toward the actual moment of friction - which is usually where the real fix lives.

Common Questions

Isn't a funnel report still a good place to start?

Yes - it's useful for narrowing down where to look closer. The mistake is treating it as the finished answer instead of the starting point for a more specific question.

How much additional tracking does this actually require?

Usually a handful of specific events tied to known friction points - shipping cost reveals, form errors, account prompts - rather than a large overhaul of existing measurement.

Does this apply to service businesses without a cart, too?

The same idea applies to any multi-step form that loses people partway through - a quote request, a booking flow, a signup process. The funnel step is still only half the picture.

Can this be set up without changing the checkout itself?

Generally, yes - most of this is additional measurement layered onto the existing flow, not a redesign of the checkout process itself.

Find Out Why They Left, Not Just Where

If the only abandonment data available today is a funnel step, that's usually a sign the actual cause hasn't been found yet.
