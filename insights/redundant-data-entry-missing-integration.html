Redundant Data Entry Is Usually a Missing Integration

By Scott Taylor

Back to Insights

Typing the same information into a second system by hand isn't a discipline problem. It's usually two systems that were never actually connected.

The Habit That Gets Blamed First

An order comes in online, and someone re-types it into the system that handles fulfillment. A new customer fills out a form, and someone re-enters their details into a separate contact list. Looked at from the outside, this reads as a process problem - someone should be more careful, or more consistent, or better trained on the correct workflow.

Looked at from the data's perspective, the same fact - already typed once, correctly, by the customer - is being retyped by hand because there's no path for it to travel from one system to the other on its own. The person doing the retyping isn't the problem. They're the workaround for a gap nobody built a bridge across.

Where the Errors Actually Come From

Every manual re-entry is a fresh opportunity for the two copies to drift apart - a typo, a dropped digit, a field left blank because it wasn't visible on the second form. None of this shows up as a dramatic failure. It shows up as a slowly growing set of small mismatches between systems that are both technically correct in isolation and quietly disagree with each other in practice.

Training reduces the rate of typos. It doesn't reduce the number of times the same fact has to be typed by hand, which is the actual source of the risk.

The Question That Finds the Real Gap

The useful question isn't "how do we get better at re-entering this." It's "why does this have to be entered twice at all." Most of the tools involved - a storefront, a scheduling system, an accounting platform - are built with a way to connect to other tools directly. The gap usually isn't a technical impossibility. It's a connection that was never set up, because the manual workaround was fast enough to ship with at the time.

That workaround being fast enough once doesn't mean it stays fast. It scales linearly with volume - twice the orders means twice the manual entry, and twice the chances for the two records to disagree.

Not Every Gap Is Worth Closing Immediately

A connection that saves ten minutes a month isn't worth building before one that's actively producing mismatched records every week. The point isn't to automate every manual step on principle - it's to recognize which manual steps are actually a missing integration wearing a training problem's clothes, and prioritize those first.

Common Questions

How do you tell a training gap from a missing integration?

Ask whether the same fact is being typed by a person into more than one system. If it is, no amount of additional training removes that repetition - only a connection between the two systems does.

Is this always worth fixing?

Not always immediately - it depends on volume and how often the manual re-entry actually produces mismatches. A rare, low-volume gap may not be worth the setup cost yet. A daily one usually is.

Does closing the gap always mean custom development?

Not necessarily - many common tools already offer a built-in way to connect to each other. The first step is usually confirming what's already possible before assuming something has to be built from scratch.

What's the risk of leaving it as-is?

Mostly a slow accumulation of small mismatches between systems that both look correct on their own - the kind of drift that's hard to notice until someone compares the two records directly and finds they don't agree.

Find the Gap Before It Finds a Customer's Order

If the same information is getting typed twice somewhere in the business, that's usually worth a closer look before the two copies quietly disagree.
